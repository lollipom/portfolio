Solo concept • Healthcare · AI · Sep 2026

A&E RapidBrief: A 30-Second Patient Briefing at Triage

Written assignment, solo · Concept designed to run on Synapxe AgentSea / Tandem · Not built or piloted

Illustrative prototype. All patients, records and sources shown are fictional. No connection to NEHR, Synapxe or any hospital.

What I heard

I spoke informally with about 15 friends working in healthcare about their A&E shifts. The same story kept coming up: during a trauma admission there's no time to dig through a patient's history. Singapore's National Electronic Health Record (NEHR) holds that history, but as raw, scattered documents. Under pressure, that's where a drug allergy or an active blood thinner gets missed.

Elsewhere, hospitals are already using AI here. In China, Ant A-Fu at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital organises patient histories and highlights urgent warning signs before a clinician begins the assessment.

Product hypothesis

If the NRIC registration that triage staff already do automatically triggers a synthesis of the patient's records into a briefing a doctor can read in about 30 seconds, with every flag linked to its source document, then critical risks are caught before invasive treatment and assessment starts sooner, with no extra input from clinicians.

I designed it to run on Synapxe's AgentSea / Tandem AI platform. That is my assumption about where it would sit, not an official project.

The doctor stays in charge

RapidBrief supports decisions, it doesn't make them. The attending doctor signs off the briefing, and that sign-off is logged.

Not yet reviewed: decision support only

Success metrics

Targets are set only after a baseline is measured, so none are claimed here.

Speed

Time to assessment

Time from NRIC registration to the doctor's first logged clinical order, taken from system timestamps.

Safety

Critical risk catch rate

Share of high-risk history (bleeding risks, allergies, blood thinners) surfaced before any invasive treatment, checked against a clinician's chart review.

Adoption

Clinician trust and repeat use

Short post-shift surveys (NASA-TLX for workload, SUS for usability) plus how often staff open the briefing shift after shift.

Guardrails: these must not get worse

  • Missed critical flags (false negatives) on allergies and blood thinners, reviewed every week.
  • Wrong-patient briefings: zero tolerance. Records are only retrieved after a second identifier matches.

Trial design

  1. Baseline first. Measure all three metrics for two weeks with no tool, then set targets.
  2. Pilot bay vs control bay. Run RapidBrief in one trauma bay while an adjacent bay with similar patient volume carries on as usual.
  3. Swap by shift. Alternate which bay has the tool, so differences in staff and case mix even out.
  4. Size it properly. Use the baseline's variation to work out how many admissions are needed before drawing conclusions.

Clinical safety and governance

  • Decision support, not diagnosis. A doctor confirms every flag against its source, and the sign-off is logged.
  • Regulation. Software that informs clinical decisions likely needs an HSA medical-device software assessment before use.
  • Approval before any pilot. Hospital ethics board and clinical governance sign-off.
  • Safe failure. If synthesis fails or is slow, staff see the normal NEHR view instantly, never a blank screen.

Key risks and how I'd test them

AI hallucination and clinical accuracy

The AI misreads a complex history or invents a detail, putting the patient at risk.

Test: offline audits comparing AI summaries against clinicians' own reviews of past records, plus a mandatory source link on every flag.

Legacy record formats and speed

Inconsistent record formats slow down retrieval just when speed matters most.

Test: sandbox stress tests under simulated load, against a generation target of under 2 seconds, to be validated.

Alert fatigue

Busy clinicians start ignoring alerts if the briefing is cluttered or flags too much.

Test: post-shift surveys and usage data from the bay trial to see where trust drops.

Roadmap, after a successful pilot

ICUs and inpatient wards

Use the same synthesis for shift handovers and ongoing patient monitoring.

Fewer forms for patients and families

Later, with consent, share only the fields each agency needs: incident details for SPF traffic reports, injury details for MOM workplace injury (WICA) claims, and support through AfterLifeSG if a family needs it.

  • Not part of the MVP and never during resuscitation. Consent is asked once the patient is stable (or from next-of-kin at discharge).
  • Run by medical social workers, not A&E doctors.
  • Inter-agency sharing is allowed under the Public Sector (Governance) Act, but public trust is the real bar.

Date our Dates: An AI Memory for Couples

Team "Dates That Stick" (3 students) · My role: [add your role]

Two sources of friction

We built around one couple who have been together for 10 years. At one date a week, that's about 520 dates' worth of memories to keep track of.

"Goldfish memory"

Couples struggle to remember which places and activities they actually enjoyed, and end up arguing over "I told you before".

Information overload

Genuine reviews and ideas are scattered across social media, mixed in with hype and paid sponsorships that don't match their shared taste.

"Sometimes she mention something during a date I thought would be nice to try next time... or even a gift idea but we forget..."

Jun Jie, one half of the couple we interviewed

The gap

There's no single, personal system that remembers what a couple enjoyed together and uses it to suggest good local plans.

An LLM connected to Notion through MCP

We designed a system where an LLM reads and writes a couple's Notion workspace through the Model Context Protocol (MCP). Quick, messy notes become rows in linked databases that the LLM can later search and plan from.

1. Capture a note

"Movie was okay, that new Thai place we tried, 'Tom Yum Palace', was amazing (9/10)! So expensive though. He mentioned wanting to try rock climbing next."

The LLM splits it into two databases:

Date Activities

Movie & Thai food · Enjoyment 6/10
Next time: rock climbing

Food & Events

Tom Yum Palace · Thai · 9/10 · $$$

2. Ask it later

"What should I get for her birthday next month?"

search-notion

Found: "She mentioned wanting a specific local ceramic mug."

For date plans it reads the taste profile with query-database and writes the plan as a new page with notion-create-pages.

Keeping recommendations fresh

A third database pulls new openings from local food guides (Eatbook and Ladyironchef) through their RSS feeds, using the third-party Note API Connector to fetch and clean the JSON, so suggestions aren't limited to places the couple already knows.

Value for couples

Fewer "I told you before" moments

Shared history is remembered for them, so less is forgotten and argued over.

Recommendations that fit

Filtered by what they actually enjoyed, not by hype or sponsored lists.

Plans ready to use

Each plan comes with budget and location links, saving time and endless scrolling.

Why it fits Notion

It runs the full Notion 3.0 AI loop through MCP (write rows, query them, publish a plan page) in a personal use case. It can ship as a template inside Notion rather than a new app, and every logged date makes the workspace more valuable to keep.

Zalora App Redesign: Decluttering the Path to Checkout

Team RE:frame (5 students) · My part: homepage, tab bar and cart/checkout prototypes in Figma

What shoppers told us

We surveyed 15 Zalora shoppers about their experience with the app. A cluttered interface came out ahead of everything else:

Cluttered, inconsistent interface13 / 15
Irrelevant results10 / 15
Discount-driven shopping9 / 15
Delivery and fulfilment issues7 / 15

Small sample, so we used it to pick the problem, not to estimate how common it is.

Why clutter matters for Zalora

A homepage packed with sale banners makes Zalora look like a discount store and buries the things that set it apart, like curated styles and its sustainability edit. That trains shoppers to come back only for discounts, which weakens loyalty.

Our direction

Reorganise the information architecture and declutter the interface so Zalora feels like a fashion brand again, with fewer, clearer paths from browsing to checkout.

These are the three parts of the app I designed. Click through the full prototype in Figma.

Open Figma prototype ↗

1. Homepage: from sale banners to three clear sections

The old homepage stacked 10.10 sale banners, partner promos and sponsored rows. I cut it to Latest Deals, Style Inspirations (curated lookbooks like "cottagecore" that open into matching products) and Sustainability Efforts, so the brand's own initiatives are visible.

2. Tab bar: wishlist moves up, messages move in

I moved Wishlist to a heart icon in the header so it's reachable from every screen, which freed a tab for Messages. The new bar is Home, Categories, Messages, Bag and Profile.

3. Cart and checkout: decide first, then pay

The old bag mixed address, vouchers, reward points and a VIP pop-up into one long scroll. I split it into two steps:

  • Cart: only the items, with a prominent checkbox per item, consistent type, and delivery dates and shipping cost per seller still visible.
  • Checkout: address, order summary and payment appear after tapping Checkout, with a simpler VIP offer and no extra pop-ups.

Testing with users

We ran low-fidelity Figma walkthroughs with the same 15 shoppers, watching how they browsed and checked out.

  • They described the redesign as "cleaner", "less cluttered" and "more premium".
  • Navigation felt intuitive and less click-heavy than the current app.
  • A common reaction: it feels more like a fashion brand and less like a discount store.

Impact hypotheses, not results

We didn't have Zalora's data, so we set targets from public benchmarks:

  • Conversion: fashion e-commerce averages about 2.9% (top performers about 3.8%). Our outside-in estimate put Zalora at 1.0–1.5%.
  • Cart abandonment: about 70% is the global average (Baymard). A cleaner cart is our bet for pulling Zalora below it.
  • How we'd measure it: event tracking and A/B flags were the first phase of our roadmap, so each change could be tested rather than projected.

Looking back [draft, rewrite in your own words]

  • We tested with the same 15 people who shaped the problem, so they were primed to like it. Next time I'd test with fresh users.
  • Cutting is harder than adding. Every banner on the old homepage was there for a reason, so the redesign had to show where each one's job went.

Scholarship Workflow: One Pipeline from Application to Offer

Track: new use case for an existing product · Team of 5 (NTU Business ×2, NTU Computer Science, NUS Industrial Design ×2) · My role: [add your role]

The brief

Find a new use case for BoB, an existing GovTech product with a configurable form engine and reusable workflow modules. We chose scholarship administration: every agency runs a similar multi-stage process, but each builds its own forms, documents and email chains from scratch.

How might we help agencies with high-volume, multi-stage scholarship workflows streamline their operations within tight timelines, by using BoB's form engine and workflow modules to unify fragmented systems and reduce manual processing?

Where the time goes

A typical public-sector scholarship cycle, based on published PSC and MOE timelines, takes up to five months:

Apply
2–3 months
Shortlist
2–4 weeks
Interview
1–2 months
Offer
1–2 weeks

The bottleneck we found by mapping the agency journey: applications arrive through a general portal, and HR has to identify each scholarship application by hand and forward it to the right department by email. Evaluators then shortlist from PDFs and share results in Excel. Nothing is tagged, so every hand-off waits on a person.

Three pain points

Fragmented intake

Applications come in by email, PDF and school referral, with no standard format or single tracker.

Manual verification

NRIC, income proof and transcripts are checked by hand, and missing documents trigger repeated requests.

Gaps between departments

No automatic routing, so departments sort and coordinate manually, causing delays and duplicated effort.

Sizing the opportunity: ~97,200 applications a year

54,000
students entering tertiary level each year (≈23k polytechnic graduates, ≈16k JC/IB, ≈15k NUS/NTU/SMU freshmen; MOE 2023)
× 60%
apply for at least one scholarship (our assumption; 90% say they intend to, Scholarship Guide 2023)
× 3
applications per applicant (our assumption; no public data exists)

An estimate, not a count. Two of the three inputs are assumptions, and JC/IB leavers partly overlap with university freshmen, so treat it as an upper bound. Even a single agency handles 1,389+ applications a year by our estimate.

Why build on BoB instead of something new

  • The form layer already exists. Today applicants submit through tools like FormSG, and the trouble starts after submission: documents, assessments and decisions scatter across email and spreadsheets. BoB already supports custom forms, Singpass retrieval, routing between departments and data export, so we built the missing workflow around it.
  • Off-the-shelf tools don't fit government. Submittable covers similar ground, but its default US/EU hosting may not meet Singapore government requirements without extra agreements.

Three modules we added

1. Applicant tracking for agencies

  • Auto-tags applications by track and eligibility, then routes them to the right department (replacing the email forwarding)
  • Bulk tagging and filtering across stages
  • Collaborative review with role-based access

Go-to-market: start where GovTech is already trusted

1

Public agencies (e.g. MOE, MINDEF, MHA)

Digitise scholarship intake, approvals and bond management in one auditable portal. Early wins establish BoB as the trusted platform for education and talent schemes.

2

Autonomous universities

Connect to each university's student information system to pre-fill student data, manage referee submissions and share review across departments.

3

Private companies and foundations

Offer a turnkey "Scholarship Kit" to corporates that sponsor tertiary students, a market tools like Workday serve rigidly and expensively today.

How we'd validate it before scaling

Pilot cohort

Run 1–2 scholarship schemes with 50–100 real applicants, randomly allocated.

Baseline vs BoB

Measure the last PDF-and-email cycle against the first BoB cycle, plus A/B sessions on non-critical awards.

Data integrity audits

Check Singpass pre-fills against records for 100 random applications, targeting zero mismatches.

Success metrics

Targets we set for the pilot, not results.

Submission to decision
8 → ≤4 weeks

≥50% faster end-to-end cycle

Staff hours per 100 applications
~40 → ≤20

Half the admin time

User satisfaction
≥4.2 / 5

Across applicants, referees and reviewers

Risks and mitigations

Integration complexity High impact

University and ministry systems use different APIs and data models, which can break pre-fill and eligibility checks.

Mitigation: agree API schemas up front, and keep a manual-upload fallback whenever an automated lookup fails.

Sensitive applicant data

GPA, household income and ID scans need government-grade data protection.

Mitigation: encryption in transit and at rest, role-based access, and regular third-party security audits.

Hesitant adoption

Administrators and reviewers may stick with familiar PDF and email workflows.

Mitigation: hands-on training, an early-adopter champion in each department, and fast UX updates from their feedback.

Bias in AI scoring Added after the hackathon

Automated scoring of scholarship candidates could encode bias against certain schools or income groups.

Mitigation: keep AI scores advisory, require a human decision with logged reasons, and audit outcomes by school and income band.

Looking back [draft, rewrite in your own words]

  • Our 97,200 estimate stacks two assumptions. Next time I'd get one agency's real intake numbers before sizing the whole market.
  • The biggest win wasn't a new feature. It was removing the manual email hand-off we found by mapping the journey.
